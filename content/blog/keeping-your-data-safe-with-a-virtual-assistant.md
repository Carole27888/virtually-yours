One of the most common worries we hear from business owners is about access. To be genuinely useful, a virtual assistant often needs to work inside your email, calendar, CRM, social media and sometimes your financial tools. That is a lot of trust to place in someone you may never meet in person.

The worry is reasonable. The good news is that with a few clear practices, you can give an assistant the access they need while keeping your information safe and staying in control. Here is how.

## Start with a written confidentiality agreement

Before anyone sees your information, there should be a signed agreement that covers confidentiality. It should state clearly that:

- Your business and client information must be kept private
- Information may only be used to do the work you have agreed
- Nothing can be shared with third parties without your permission
- These obligations continue after the working relationship ends

If you work with an agency, ask whether every assistant signs one before starting, and whether the agency itself has an agreement with you.

## Never share passwords by email or chat

Passwords sent by email or messaging apps can stay in those conversations for years. If either account is ever compromised, those passwords are exposed.

Instead, use a password manager. Most popular password managers let you share a login with someone so they can sign in without ever seeing the actual password. You can also remove their access at any time with a single click, without changing the password everywhere.

## Use delegated access where it exists

Many tools have a built-in way to give someone access to your account without sharing your login at all. Email providers, calendars, social media platforms and many business tools offer this.

Delegated access is better than sharing a login because:

- The assistant uses their own account, so you can see what they did
- You can limit what they are allowed to do
- Removing access does not affect your own login

Where a tool offers this option, use it.

## Turn on two-step verification everywhere

Two-step verification means that signing in requires something more than a password, usually a code from an app or a text message. It is one of the most effective ways to protect any account.

Make sure it is turned on for your email, your password manager, your banking and payment tools and any account that holds client information. When an assistant needs to sign in to an account protected this way, agree on how the code will be handled, ideally through the password manager or an authenticator app you control.

## Give only the access that is needed

It is easy to grant full access to everything just to save time. Resist that. Instead, think about each task and what it genuinely requires.

- An assistant managing your calendar does not need access to your bank account
- An assistant posting on social media may only need publishing rights, not admin rights
- An assistant handling invoices may need to create them, but not to change your payment details

Starting with limited access and adding more when needed is far safer than the other way around.

## Keep everything in your own accounts

Documents, contacts, templates and anything your assistant creates should live in your accounts, not theirs. Use shared folders that you own, and make sure your CRM, social media and website logins are registered to your business.

This matters for two reasons. First, you always have access to your own information. Second, if the working relationship ends, nothing is stuck in someone else's account.

## Agree on what is off limits

Some information is more sensitive than the rest. Agree in writing on anything your assistant should never access or act on without asking first. Examples include:

- Personal or family email folders
- Payroll and HR records
- Changes to bank details
- Payments above a certain amount
- Replies to complaints or legal matters

Clear boundaries protect both of you.

## Review access regularly

Every few months, take ten minutes to look at who has access to what. Remove anything that is no longer needed. Tools and tasks change over time, and access often builds up without anyone noticing.

## Have a clean handover plan

Before you start, agree what will happen if you stop working together. A good handover includes:

- Removing all access on the same day
- Returning or confirming the location of all files
- Changing any passwords that may have been seen
- A written confirmation that nothing has been kept

Planning this at the start makes the end straightforward, whenever it comes.

## A quick security checklist

- Signed confidentiality agreement
- Password manager for all shared logins
- Delegated access wherever possible
- Two-step verification on key accounts
- Only the access each task needs
- All files kept in your own accounts
- Written list of what is off limits
- Access reviewed every few months
- Handover plan agreed in advance
